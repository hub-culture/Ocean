Friday was AI, Zeke and global commons day at the Climate Campus. The Global Commons Initiative, first sketched on Wednesday, moved from ideas to working tools: a library of what we already know about shared resources, a charter that lets any community set its own rules, and the ocean as the first place to use them.

Start from what already works

The Global Commons starts from a simple idea: shared resources like the ocean, the atmosphere and nature's medicines already have some rules. Where they are failing, it's because those rules are missing, too weak, or don't reward the people who look after them.

The Nobel-winning economist Elinor Ostrom showed that communities can manage shared resources well, without either a state or a market taking over, and found eight things that long-lasting commons have in common: clear boundaries, rules made by the people who use the resource, cheap ways to check that everyone keeps to them, fair penalties, and a few more. On day three, we tested Hub Culture's own platforms against her list. Almost every point already has a working part, from Gaia's shared map to Propel's community votes. The job now is to connect them.

The Library of the Commons

[image]

Ostrom didn't work alone. Over four decades, the workshop she co-founded at Indiana University trained and gathered a generation of researchers who tested her ideas in forests, fisheries, irrigation systems, cities and grazing lands around the world.

The Library of the Commons maps that lineage for the first time: 149 scholars, from 54 PhDs on commons and collective action, to 48 on wider questions of how institutions work, to 47 affiliated faculty. Each entry notes the scholar's research focus and links to its source. One example from the first pass: Esther Mwangi's 2003 study of property rights in Kenya's Maasailand, the same landscape where a herder once offered his cattle for a car, a story from Wednesday's session about how wrong outside assumptions can be.

The Library has two jobs. It's a brain trust: the people best placed to correct and extend the Global Commons framework. And it's a reference that AI can read in full, so that Zeke can give answers grounded in decades of field evidence and still specific to one place. The map is open, and scholars are invited to add to it.

The commons charter

Ostrom's central finding was that the best rules are written by the people who use a resource, and that they are almost always local. So the Global Commons doesn't impose one rulebook. Every community that looks after a commons, whether a marine protected area, a forest or a network of AI agents, writes its own charter:

  • Where it is and who belongs. Its boundary on the shared Gaia map, and how people join.
  • How decisions are made. Members change the rules through Propel votes, with the majorities the community chooses.
  • How money flows. A share of activity goes into a community fund, released to projects in stages as work is done.
  • Rules for AI agents. A community can charge agents a fee to operate under its rules. Agents put up a deposit; if one breaks the rules, it can lose part of that deposit to the community, with the right to appeal.
  • A shared floor. The Bermuda Standard sets the defaults any charter starts from, so a new community doesn't begin from a blank page, and charters can nest inside one another, from a single reef up to the ocean as a whole.

Many commons sit where the internet is weakest, so each community can also switch on Hub Culture's offline mesh network through its hub, letting a fishing village or a forest ranger post report, vote and get paid, and sync later.

The ocean first: an open blue carbon registry

Mangroves, salt marshes and seagrass store huge amounts of carbon, and they're still being lost. Wednesday's proposal for an open blue carbon registry is now a working draft: a free registry where anyone can list one of these sites. Each claim about a site, such as its size or the carbon it holds, is checked against independent public data before it counts. The registry doesn't sell carbon credits. It builds the evidence that a site deserves protection and funding, and the Ocean Climate Fund can then help pay for what's missing. Each site can then become a commons of its own, with its own charter.

Better climate data, in real time

Every decision above depends on data people can trust. The Ocean Data Initiative, launched on Wednesday, is open to anyone who needs it:

  • Find it. Sea temperature, coral reef heat stress, sargassum, microplastics and ocean finance for the whole Atlantic Rim, refreshed every week, at oceanclimatefund.com/data and on GitHub.
  • Trust it. Every reading is checked against independent sources, and labelled certain, uncertain or contradicted, so you know how far to rely on it.
  • Use it. Drop the map layers straight into your own GIS tool, dashboard or app, or ask Zeke on Telegram about what the data shows.
  • Add to it. Know a source that would make the picture better, such as a local buoy, a mangrove survey or a monitoring programme? Propose it, and it can be checked and added for everyone.
